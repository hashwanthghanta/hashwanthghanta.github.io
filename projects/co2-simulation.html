Visualising Post-Combustion CO₂ Capture, End-to-End

A five-person team built an interactive Unity simulation of a 1000 MW coal-fired power plant's post-combustion CO₂ capture unit — modelled in Blender, scripted in C#, validated against real chemical-engineering calculations. My role: Unity, Blender & UI Developer. I built the 3D plant models, the Unity simulation behaviour, every screen and interface, the entire demo screen, and the walkthrough video. Coursework for the M.Sc. Digital Engineering programme at OVGU Magdeburg.

The brief

OVGU's Digital Engineering Project module asks teams to take a complex industrial process and make it interactively understandable — not a static diagram, not a pre-rendered animation, but something a student or engineer can poke at and immediately see the consequences.

Our brief was post-combustion CO₂ capture for a coal-fired power plant — chemical absorption with monoethanolamine (MEA), modelled against a 1000 MW reference plant. The output had to be installable on Windows, recorded as a guided demo video with English captions and German subtitles, and supported by an engineering-calculation report. Supervised by Dr.-Ing. David Broneske (Computer Science) and Dr.-Ing. Nicole Vorhauer (Process Engineering).

A five-person collaboration across two departments. The chemical engineers owned the process design and the equations behind every threshold in the simulation; the digital engineers owned the 3D models, the Unity scenes, the C# behaviour, and the user-facing interfaces.

Credit where it's due

The chemical process flow — the order of operations, the unit-by-unit mass-balance assumptions, the temperature thresholds — came from Salman and Rony. Without that work the simulation would be animation, not a model.

The process, in one paragraph

Flue gas from a coal-fired power plant enters the absorber column from the bottom and flows up. A 30 wt% MEA solvent showers down from the top in counter-current contact, chemically absorbing the CO₂ to form rich MEA. The rich MEA is pumped through a heat exchanger into the stripper column, where a reboiler heats it enough to release the captured CO₂ as a concentrated gas stream. The regenerated lean MEA loops back to the absorber, and the released CO₂ is sent to the storage tank for compression and downstream use. The whole system depends on temperature — too hot in the absorber and the solvent stops absorbing, too cold in the stripper and it won't release what it captured.

Engineering basis — the real numbers

Every dimension in the 3D scene and every threshold in the C# scripts traces back to the chemical engineers' calculation report. The basis:

Reference plant1000 MW net coal-fired power plant
Solvent30 wt% Monoethanolamine (MEA) in water
Capture target90% of inlet CO₂
Flue gas flow3,500,000 Nm³/h (43.41 kmol/s)
Captured CO₂232 kg/s → ≈ 6.68 Mt / year (8000 op. hours)
Absorber height30 m (NTU = 15, HTU = 2 m)
Stripper height40 m (NTU = 20, HTU = 2 m)
Regeneration duty≈ 3.8 GJ / t CO₂ (in line with industrial benchmarks)
How the numbers were picked

The 30 m absorber height isn't an aesthetic choice — it's NTU × HTU. The 125 °C overheat threshold isn't arbitrary either — it's the temperature at which MEA starts to degrade and the stripper efficiency curve breaks down in literature.

Architecture

The application ships as four Unity scenes — splash, main menu, simulation, and demo — driven by 11 C# scripts. The simulation scene is the engine of the project: one central SimulationManager runs the full process as a coroutine called RunSimulationSequence(), and a handful of supporting managers each own one concern (audio, notifications, hover tooltips, the efficiency graph, particle systems, the overheat watchdog).

The seven phases of RunSimulationSequence()

One coroutine drives the entire process. Each phase yields with a custom WaitWithChecks() so timing pauses cleanly whenever the user hits Pause — or whenever the overheat watchdog fires.

  1. MEA Shower: lean solvent begins flowing into the top of the absorber.
  2. Flue Gas Intake: CO₂-rich flue gas enters the absorber from the bottom.
  3. Absorption: MEA reacts with CO₂; particle colours and emission rates update with the absorber slider.
  4. Solvent Transfer: rich MEA pumped across the heat exchanger to the stripper.
  5. Stripping: reboiler heats the rich MEA; white steam rises, CO₂ is released.
  6. Compression & Storage: released CO₂ is spawned as 3D molecules filling the storage tank.
  7. Lean MEA Return: regenerated solvent loops back to the absorber for the next cycle.

Decisions and tradeoffs

Reference-table efficiency vs. continuous model

The chemical engineers gave us a nine-point reference table for capture efficiency vs. temperature (absorber: 97% at 25 °C → 61.8% at 65 °C; stripper: 78% at 85 °C → 94.5% at 110 °C). The original idea was to fit a polynomial and compute live. We shipped the lookup table instead — fast on WebGL, exact at the supervisor's reference points, and the on-graph disclaimer told users it's a teaching aid, not an engineering model.

Takeaway

For an educational tool, deterministic reference data beats a polynomial fit that quietly hides off-spec behaviour. The on-graph disclaimer made the scope of the model explicit instead of pretending the lookup table was a continuous physics engine.

Blender first, Unity second

All six equipment components — absorber, stripper, reboiler, heat exchanger, compressor, CO₂ storage tank — were modelled in Blender 4.x before any of them touched Unity. Internal lattice packing inside the columns was built as a repeating mesh pattern; pipes were created via Blender's curve-to-mesh workflow so the bends were smooth without manual polygon editing. Each model exported as FBX with Apply Transform enabled — without that flag, Blender's Z-up coordinate system arrives in Unity's Y-up world rotated 90 ° and scaled 1/100.

UI Toolkit (UXML/USS) for menus, UGUI for the simulation

Main menu and demo screen are built with Unity's newer UI Toolkit because the layouts are mostly declarative and benefit from USS styling. The simulation scene stays on the older UGUI Canvas system because it needs world-space depth sorting and TextMeshPro labels welded to 3D objects — both of which UI Toolkit doesn't handle as cleanly yet. Mixing them in the same project felt wrong on paper, was the right call in practice.

One simulation scene, not seven

Early experiment: one Unity scene per phase. It looked clean until the moment we needed continuous animation between phases (rich MEA flowing from absorber to stripper isn't a scene change — it's the point). A single simulation scene with phase-keyed visibility groups solved both the transition problem and the asset duplication problem in one move.

Things that broke (and how)

Worth documenting because every Unity-on-WebGL project hits these eventually:

  • Unicode glyphs vanished in the standalone build. The close-button ✕ rendered fine in the Editor and disappeared in the .exe. Unity's default font atlas strips uncommon Unicode at build time. Fix: replace with ASCII X.
  • FBX imported at 1/100 scale. Blender's FBX exporter applies a 0.01 unit factor by default. Apply Transform in the export settings bakes the correct scale in.
  • UI Image overlays drew on top of 3D, never inside it. Canvas elements always paint over the scene. For the equipment-highlight effect we used a material with renderQueue = 4000 so it participates in depth sorting instead.
  • Efficiency graph stayed tiny after the window was maximised. Anchors had drifted. Reset GraphArea RectTransform to full stretch (0,0 → 1,1), then call ShowGraph() after a 2-frame WaitForEndOfFrame so Unity's layout system finishes its recalculation first.
  • Notification cards stacked on top of each other under fast phase transitions. Calling RepositionCards() on every add/remove fixed the vertical layout.
  • UI Toolkit tooltips refused to render. The standard tooltip API didn't fire in our scene. Re-implemented with MouseEnterEvent / MouseLeaveEvent callbacks — same visible result, different code path.
  • PlasticSCM workspace hang. Deleting four root files — plastic.selector, plastic.wktree, plastic.changes, plastic.workspace — reopened the workspace cleanly.

The demo video

A 2 minute 57.5 second video ships with the build. Unity Recorder captured the simulation at 1080p H.264 with seven scripted segments matched to the on-screen highlights (38 s intro, 23.5 s absorber, 16 s heat exchanger, 19.5 s stripper, 14 s reboiler, 32 s compressor + storage, 34.5 s conclusion). An animated green-screen presenter from Animaker was composited in over the Unity footage in Adobe Premiere Pro via Ultra Key. English captions were auto-generated with Premiere's Speech-to-Text and reviewed by hand; German subtitles came from a separately authored SRT file imported as its own track.

What I'd do differently

  • Replace the 9-point lookup with a polynomial regression across the full operating range of both sliders. The discrete table works but the on-graph disclaimer would be unnecessary with a smooth fit.
  • Port the simulation to VR. Walking around a 30 m absorber column at scale would land harder than seeing it on a 24" monitor.
  • Hook a live plant data stream into the existing manager pattern so the same UI could double as a monitoring dashboard. The data flow is already one-way through SimulationManager — the seam exists.
  • Expand EnhancedHoverTooltip to all six equipment components. Right now it covers a subset.
