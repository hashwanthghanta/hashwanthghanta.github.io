Smart Helmet — Gated Ignition + Auto-SOS

Two Arduino-class units talking over RF. The helmet won't let the bike start unless it's actually being worn by a sober rider. If the bike vibrates hard enough to register a crash, the bike unit dispatches the rider's GPS coordinates over GSM to a pre-saved emergency contact. Solo Bachelor's senior project for VIT-AP University, Electronics & Communication Engineering, submitted May 2024.

Role: Solo · sensors, firmware, integration Stack: Arduino + NodeMCU · ATmega328P · Embedded C Supervisor: Dr. Chandan Nayak Status: Working prototype · degree-submitted
Honest framing

This is a 2024 undergraduate project — not the kind of work I'm pitching for working-student roles in 2026. I'm including it because the sensor-gating + alert-pipeline thinking still maps cleanly to any system where a physical condition must hold before software allows an action. Read it as "what I learned about hardware-software boundaries", not "look what I built last week."

The motivation

India records roughly 1.5 lakh (150,000) two-wheeler road deaths a year — the WHO consistently rates it the worst per-capita rate globally. Section 129 of India's Motor Vehicle Act 1988 makes a helmet legally mandatory; in practice enforcement is patchy and a large share of fatal crashes involve riders who either weren't wearing one or had been drinking. A traditional helmet is passive — it protects in the crash but doesn't change whether the bike starts in the first place. The thesis asks: what if the helmet itself were the ignition key?

What it does

Pre-ignitionBike refuses to start unless (a) helmet is on the rider's head AND (b) breath-alcohol is below the threshold.
During rideContinuous monitoring of helmet seat, alcohol sensor, and vibration channel on the bike side.
On impactVibration spike above threshold → bike auto-stops, GPS coordinates captured, GSM SMS dispatched to pre-saved emergency number.
VisibilityLED strip on the rear of the helmet shell for night riding.

The two-unit architecture

The system splits into a helmet unit (sensors + transmitter, battery-powered, wearable form factor) and a bike unit (receiver + GSM + GPS + relay into the ignition circuit). They talk over a 433 MHz RF link rather than Bluetooth, because the bike unit needs to keep listening even with the helmet hung on the handlebar a metre away in a noisy 2.4 GHz urban environment.

The decision logic

On every loop, the bike unit asks three questions before allowing ignition:

  1. Is the helmet on a head? The FSR (force-sensing resistor) inside the helmet's crown padding only registers pressure when the helmet is actually being worn — not when it's dangling from the handlebar. Reading below threshold → ignition stays locked.
  2. Is the rider sober? The MQ-3 alcohol sensor sits just below the face shield. The thesis sets the alarm threshold at 0.04 mg/L — half of India's legal 0.08 mg/L cutoff — so the demo trips noticeably before the legal limit. Reading above threshold → ignition stays locked.
  3. Is the crash channel quiet? The vibration sensor's running frequency stays below a calibrated noise floor during normal riding. A spike above the threshold flips the unit into accident mode.

If (1) AND (2) hold and (3) is quiet → the relay closes and the bike starts. Any of those flipping during a ride re-evaluates the gate.

The accident pipeline

On a vibration spike above the calibrated threshold, the bike unit treats it as an impact. It cuts the relay (engine off), reads the latest GPS coordinates the receiver has buffered, formats them as a short SMS, and pushes the message through the GSM modem to a phone number stored in the firmware at flash time. The LCD shows the same coordinates so a bystander can read them out loud if needed. The transmission repeats periodically until the bike is manually power-cycled, in case the first SMS fails to reach the tower.

Why GSM + GPS, not just a phone tether

A phone in the rider's pocket can be unconscious, cracked, or locked. The bike unit needs to alert without anyone touching anything. GSM + GPS bolted to the bike's own battery survives anything that doesn't take the bike itself out.

What I'd do differently today

  • Drop the GSM modem for an ESP32 + LTE-M / NB-IoT module. GSM 2G is being shut down in most markets. A modern cellular IoT module is smaller, cheaper, and has a future.
  • Replace the 0.04 mg/L hardcoded threshold with a calibration routine. The MQ-3 drifts with temperature, humidity, and age — a one-time field calibration in the firmware setup would make it production-realistic.
  • Buffer GPS coordinates locally before the crash. Today's pipeline reads "current" GPS at SOS time, which can be stale or unfixed. Logging the last 30 seconds would let the SMS include the trajectory, not just a point.
  • Add a fallback BLE link to a paired phone app. If the rider's phone is intact, it can echo the SOS over Wi-Fi or 4G, and a confirmation back from the phone tells the unit "message received, you can stop retransmitting."
  • Treat the ignition bypass risk seriously. Anyone with a screwdriver can short the relay. Real production would need a tamper switch and either a CAN-bus interlock (modern bikes) or a permanently-wired immobiliser circuit.

What this project taught me

  • Hardware boundaries don't lie. An if-statement in firmware can disable the ignition only if the relay is physically in series with the starter circuit. Software gates without physical gates are theatre.
  • Calibrate, then commit. The MQ-3 needed a 24-hour warm-up before its readings stabilised — the firmware did not. First demo failed because of it. Calibration is part of the design, not an afterthought.
  • Optimise for the failure mode, not the happy path. A working ignition gate gets you a passing demo. A working SOS pipeline is what justifies the whole project. I'd build the SOS path first if I did this again.
